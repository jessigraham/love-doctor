            Grow toward secure

            Attachment styles aren't a life sentence. Researchers call it earned security: adults who started out anxious, avoidant, or disorganized and became secure. Pick a starting point to see the path from there.

              Try this week:

              How people become secure

              • A steady relationship. A secure partner, close friend, or mentor who stays consistent slowly rewires what you expect from closeness.
              • Therapy. Attachment-focused approaches like Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), schema therapy, and trauma-informed work are built for this.
              • Making sense of your story. People who can tell a clear, honest story about their childhood, even a hard one, tend to be more secure as adults.
              • Practice in small moments. Every time you ask directly instead of testing, or stay instead of fleeing, it gets a little easier.

              Choosing a better match

              • Look for consistency. They text when they say they will, make plans, and their actions match their words.
              • Notice how they handle your feelings. A good match gets curious when you're upset instead of defensive or distant.
              • Watch how they repair. Everyone fights. What matters is whether they apologize and come back.
              • Question the spark. If kind, steady people feel boring, that may be your nervous system missing the anxiety, not missing chemistry.

              Building security as a couple

              • Name the cycle, not the person. “We're doing the chase-and-pull-away thing again” makes it a shared problem.
              • Agree on a repair ritual. A phrase or gesture that means “I want to fix this,” used by whoever cools down first.
              • Hold a weekly check-in. What felt good this week, what felt off, and one thing each of you needs.
              • Get outside help early. Couples therapy works best before resentment sets in, not as a last resort.

              The four styles

              Attachment theory comes from John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth's work on how children bond with caregivers. Researchers later found adults carry similar patterns into romance. Most people sit somewhere on two scales: how much they fear losing closeness (anxiety) and how much they avoid it (avoidance).

              This is a tool for self-reflection, not a diagnosis. Attachment styles are tendencies, not fixed labels, and they can shift over time. Many people move toward "earned security" through supportive relationships or therapy. Scores here are general tendencies drawn from attachment research; any two people who are self-aware and willing to work on it can build something good.
